You rake the leaves off the drain. The grate looks clear. Then it rains again, and the same puddle spreads across the driveway.

At a house, that water may creep toward the garage. At a business, it may cover the walkway customers use or collect where deliveries unload. Either way, cleaning what you can see has not answered the important question: **where is the water getting held up?**

An outdoor drain that is not draining can have a blocked inlet, a basin full of sediment, a restricted underground pipe, or a problem where the water is supposed to discharge. Sometimes the trouble is the surrounding grade or the amount of runoff, rather than a clog. The next step depends on which part is failing.

## A clear grate is only the beginning

Think of an outdoor drainage system as a route. Water needs to enter, travel through the connected pipe, and reach a place that can accept it. A clean opening tells you about only the first part.

**Follow the water: surface → inlet or basin → connecting pipe → discharge point.**

Your property may have a long channel drain across the driveway, a small yard drain, or several catch basins around a parking lot. The layouts differ, but the useful question stays the same: which part of the route is not doing its job?

### 1. The inlet: can water reach the opening?

A mat of wet leaves can cover a grate. Sand and mulch can fill a narrow channel. Water may also bypass an otherwise clear drain if the pavement or soil directs runoff somewhere else.

From a safe, dry spot, look at where the puddle forms relative to the drain. Is the water sitting directly over it, or collecting several feet away without reaching it? That distinction helps separate an intake problem from a surface-drainage problem.

### 2. The basin: what is collecting below the grate?

A catch basin commonly has a low section, called a sump, where sediment settles below the outlet pipe. The basin can need cleaning even when the grate is spotless.

[Rhode Island DEM's catch basin guidance](https://dem.ri.gov/media/29411/download) explains that sediment and debris must be removed periodically to help prevent blockages and reduce pollution reaching waterways. Clearing the surface does not remove the material stored underneath it.

Also, **water visible inside a catch basin does not automatically mean it is clogged**. A sumped basin may retain water below its outlet. Overflow, sediment accumulation, and the way the connected line drains are more useful clues than the mere presence of water.

### 3. The pipe: can water leave the drain?

The opening can be clear while the buried line is restricted by sediment, debris, or roots. Damage to a pipe can also interfere with drainage. Those possibilities cannot be distinguished just by looking at a puddle.

If clearing loose surface debris makes little difference—or the same problem returns—have the connected line assessed. Avoid pushing a rod or pressure-washer hose into an unknown pipe. The material, condition, route, and endpoint affect which cleaning method is appropriate.

### 4. The discharge point: where does the water go?

Some systems discharge through a visible outlet or pop-up emitter. Others connect to a larger drainage system or an infiltration structure. Do not assume you know the destination because the pipe disappears underground.

A blocked outlet, a downstream restriction, or an infiltration area that cannot accept more water can leave an upstream drain full. Cleaning the inlet alone will not resolve those conditions. If the destination is unknown, ask about tracing the route and checking the outlet as part of the visit.

## What the puddle can tell you

Use these observations to describe the problem. They are clues for a service visit, not a diagnosis from a distance.


| What you notice | What needs checking |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Leaves cover the grate, and water pools directly over it | The inlet and any debris immediately underneath |
| The grate is clear, but the drain fills and overflows | Sediment in the basin, the connected pipe, and the discharge route |
| Water collects beside the drain without reaching it | Pavement or soil slope, inlet position, and the path of runoff |
| One drain overflows when another nearby drain receives water | Whether they share a restricted downstream line |
| The problem appeared after landscaping or paving | Changed runoff paths, buried outlets, and possible construction debris or damage |
| Flooding occurs only during unusually intense rain | Both maintenance condition and the system's capacity; a clean drain can still be overwhelmed |


Try to note whether this is new behavior. A driveway that drained well for years and now floods after ordinary rain presents a different starting point from a low area that has always held water.

## What you can safely do before service

- **Gather observations.** From a dry, safe location, photograph where water collects and note whether it happens during light rain, heavy rain, or both.
- **Remove loose surface litter when conditions are safe.** Stay on your property, away from traffic and floodwater. Collect leaves and debris; do not sweep them through the grate.
- **Keep access available.** Move parked vehicles from service areas when safe, and tell the technician about locked gates or known underground utilities near the work area.
- **Leave covers and basin entry to professionals.** Heavy grates, hidden openings, and underground structures create hazards that are easy to underestimate.
- **Skip chemical drain cleaners.** Do not pour them, wash water, or other waste into an outdoor storm drain.

Rhode Island DEM explains that storm drains can carry pollution directly to local waterways. Its [stormwater guidance](https://dem.ri.gov/ri-stormwater-solutions/violations.php) specifically identifies yard waste, soapy water, oil, and other wastes as materials that do not belong there. Outdoor cleaning should remove debris from the system, not wash it farther downstream.

If the affected inlet is in a public street, report it to the municipality responsible for that roadway. Do not remove a street grate or attempt work in traffic. A private-property service visit cannot promise to resolve a restriction in public infrastructure.

## What should exterior drain cleaning include?

Ask for a scope that follows the problem through the system. Depending on the layout and findings, that may include clearing the inlet, removing basin sediment, cleaning the connecting line, locating an outlet, and inspecting accessible pipe when needed.

Then ask how the result will be checked. A controlled water test can show how the serviced section flows at the time of the visit. It does not reproduce every storm or guarantee that the site can handle any amount of rainfall.

If a cleaned line still does not drain properly, the next conversation should address the remaining evidence. Damaged pipe, an unsuitable slope, or a downstream capacity problem may call for further evaluation. Repeating the same cleaning without understanding why water remains is not a maintenance plan.

## How often should outdoor drains be cleaned?

Base the schedule on what the property collects and what previous visits find. A drain under mature trees, a sandy parking lot, and a sheltered patio do not receive the same debris load.

For Rhode Island properties, checking surface inlets during leaf fall and after winter sand accumulates is a practical starting point. Check again after landscaping or paving changes. Those checks help identify when professional service is needed; they do not replace inspection of the basin and line.

Commercial sites with repeat trouble can use service records to set a more useful schedule. Ask about a [drain maintenance program](https://honestdrainandsewer.com/commercial-service/maintenance-programs/) based on the property's history and any applicable site maintenance plan.

## Common questions about outdoor drains

### Can hydro jetting fix an outdoor drain that keeps flooding?

It can help with suitable blockages in suitable pipe. It will not change the slope of the parking lot, repair a broken pipe, or make a saturated discharge area absorb more water. The system should be assessed before choosing the equipment.

### Is catch basin cleaning the same as storm drain cleaning?

The terms can overlap in conversation. Catch basin cleaning generally focuses on material collected in the inlet structure and sump; storm drain work may also involve the connected lines. Confirm the structures and pipe sections included in the quote.

### How much does outdoor drain cleaning cost in Rhode Island?

The scope depends on access, basin size and count, debris volume, pipe length, and any inspection or outlet locating required. Describe those details when requesting a quote. Ask whether material removal, line cleaning, and flow testing are included so you can compare like-for-like work.

### Should a clear drain eliminate every puddle?

No. Low pavement, runoff that bypasses the inlet, and drainage capacity can still cause pooling. Cleaning is useful when a restriction is part of the problem. Persistent water after cleaning deserves an explanation of what remains unresolved.
